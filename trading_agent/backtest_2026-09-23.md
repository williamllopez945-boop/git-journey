# Strategy backtest — 2026-09-23

Real historical data, not synthetic. Crypto has no historicals source (see
README's "Crypto price history" section) — the RobinHood scanner only gives
a current-moment SMA snapshot, not a retrievable time series, and external
data sources (CoinGecko, Binance) are blocked by this environment's network
policy. Used **IBIT** (iShares Bitcoin Trust) and **ETHA** (iShares Ethereum
Trust) instead — equity ETFs that track BTC/ETH price closely, with real
historicals available via `get_equity_historicals`.

**Caveat:** these are proxies, not crypto itself. They trade only during
equity market hours (not 24/7 like crypto), and carry their own tracking
error/premium-discount vs. NAV. Treat this as a real but imperfect signal
on the strategy's edge, not a guarantee of crypto performance.

## Setup

- 738 hourly bars each, 2026-03-27 to 2026-09-22 (~180 days)
- Exact production code: `strategy.sma_crossover_signal` (SMA 10/30) +
  `exit_criteria.check_exit` (10% stop-loss, 15% take-profit sells 80%)
- Position sizing: 100% of available cash per trade (not the live agent's
  5%-of-portfolio cap) — isolates entry/exit signal quality from the
  risk-management layer. Real $ impact under the live 5% cap is ~1/20th
  of the returns below.

## Results

| | IBIT | ETHA |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy return | **+6.40%** | **+25.19%** |
| Buy & hold return | +31.17% | +38.91% |
| Strategy max drawdown | 15.02% | 11.16% |
| Buy & hold max drawdown | 28.87% | 36.73% |
| Trades executed | 30 | 27 |
| Round trips | 15 | 14 |
| Win rate | 40.0% | 42.9% |

## Reading this honestly

**The strategy underperformed simple buy-and-hold in both cases** — by a
lot on IBIT (+6.4% vs +31.2%), less severely on ETHA (+25.2% vs +38.9%).
It did cut drawdown roughly in half in both cases, so this is a real
risk/return tradeoff, not just a worse strategy — but the tradeoff is
steep, especially on IBIT.

**Why:** most round trips were small losses from the raw SMA(10,30)
crossover firing on noise — whipsaws where price crossed back within a
few bars, exiting at close to breakeven-to-small-loss via the death
cross. IBIT's round-trip P/Ls:
`[2.38, -3.57, -3.05, -0.77, 0.3, 2.23, -2.63, -0.71, -2.38, -3.16, 0.03, 19.83, 21.96, -2.9, -4.23]`
— 9 of 15 trades lost 0.7-4.2%, and almost all the strategy's total
return came from just 2 trades (+19.83%, +21.96%).

**The take-profit rule earned its place.** Every take-profit trigger in
both samples was followed by the remaining 20% riding to an even larger
gain before its eventual death-cross exit — e.g. ETHA: take-profit at
+15.23%, then the remaining 20% closed later at +11.27%; take-profit at
+19.48%, remaining 20% closed at +29.31%. The "sell 80%, let 20% ride"
design (from the earlier strategy discussion) meaningfully outperformed
what a hard 100%-exit-at-take-profit rule would have captured.

## What this suggests for next steps

The whipsaw losses are the strategy's biggest weakness in this sample —
consistent with the entry-confirmation and whipsaw-cooldown gaps flagged
in the prior strategy discussion, not yet built. Worth prioritizing:
1. A confirmation filter on entries (minimum crossover strength, or a
   volume check) to reduce trading on marginal/noisy crosses.
2. A cooldown after a stop-loss or death-cross exit before re-entering
   the same asset, to avoid repeated whipsaw losses in a choppy stretch.

Re-running this same backtest after either change (just re-run
`backtest.py` against `/tmp/historicals.json` or fetch fresh bars) is the
way to check whether a proposed change actually helps, instead of
guessing.

## Follow-up: entry confirmation filter (same day)

Built `entry_filter.py` and swept `min_strength_pct` against these same
IBIT/ETHA series to pick a default empirically rather than guessing.

**First attempt failed informatively:** requiring crossover strength *at
the instant of the cross* killed every single trade at any threshold
≥0.5% — zero trades fired. This makes sense in hindsight: by definition,
the two SMAs are nearly equal exactly when they cross, so a strength
requirement checked at that instant rejects nearly all real crosses, not
just noisy ones. Redesigned to check one cycle *after* the cross instead
(does the new state still hold, and has the gap grown by then), which is
what actually distinguishes a persisting move from a whipsaw.

**Sweep results (1-bar persistence + strength, evaluated post-cross):**

| Threshold | IBIT return | IBIT max DD | IBIT win rate | ETHA return | ETHA max DD | ETHA win rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| unfiltered | +6.40% | 15.02% | 40.0% | +25.19% | 11.16% | 42.9% |
| 0% (persistence only) | +19.20% | 10.00% | 35.7% | +28.00% | 13.51% | 53.8% |
| 0.1% | +22.04% | 9.36% | 50.0% | +23.11% | 12.37% | 45.5% |
| **0.25%** | **+31.00%** | **7.46%** | **66.7%** | **+27.01%** | **10.26%** | **55.6%** |
| 0.5% | -4.88% | 20.18% | 50.0% | +1.95% | 4.84% | 0.0% |
| ≥1.0% | 0 trades | — | — | 0 trades | — | — |

**0.25% is the default now** (`entry_filter.DEFAULT_MIN_STRENGTH_PCT`) —
best-or-near-best on both assets, with meaningfully better drawdown and
win rate than unfiltered on both. Even the persistence check alone (0%)
captured most of the improvement; strength added a further edge. Above
0.5%, filtering becomes too aggressive and results collapse — consistent
with the earlier finding that over-filtering costs more than it saves.

Wired into both live detection paths: `scanner_signals.classify` (the
fast, scanner-based path — a cross is now held "pending" for one cycle
before confirming) and the `price_history.py` polling path (now uses
`entry_filter.confirmed_signal` instead of the raw
`strategy.sma_crossover_signal`). See `PLAYBOOK.md`.

## Follow-up: whipsaw cooldown (same day)

Built `position_state.py`'s `record_exit`/`in_cooldown` and swept
`cooldown_bars` in `backtest.py` against the same IBIT/ETHA data, with
the 0.25% entry filter already applied as the baseline — to check whether
a cooldown adds anything on top of the entry filter, not instead of it.

| Cooldown | IBIT return | IBIT max DD | IBIT win rate | ETHA return | ETHA max DD | ETHA win rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| none (0.25% filter only) | +31.00% | 7.46% | 66.7% | +27.01% | 10.26% | 55.6% |
| 10-20h | +31.00% | 7.46% | 66.7% | **+37.83%** | **8.70%** | **71.4%** |
| 22h+ | +34.99% | 7.46% | 80.0% | +37.83% | 8.70% | 71.4% |
| 48h | +34.99% | 7.46% | 80.0% | +10.19% | 12.64% | 50.0% |

**12 hours is the default** (`position_state.DEFAULT_COOLDOWN_HOURS`) —
inside the 10-20h plateau where ETHA gets its full benefit and IBIT is
unaffected either way. 22h+ improves IBIT slightly further (a single
fewer trade in this sample - too thin to trust over 12h) but starts
costing ETHA past ~24h (peaks at +37.83%, falls to +10.19% by 48h as the
cooldown starts blocking genuinely good re-entries, not just whipsaws).
12h captures the real, robust improvement without extrapolating from a
small-sample artifact.

Wired into both live detection paths via `PositionStateStore`: a full
exit (stop-loss or death cross closing a position to zero) calls
`record_exit(asset)` in addition to `reset(asset)`; a new-entry signal
first checks `in_cooldown(asset)` and is skipped entirely - not
downgraded to a recommendation - if still within the window. See
`PLAYBOOK.md`'s "Per-position exit rules" and step 5d / the scanner
cycle's step 4.

## Follow-up: broader backtest reveals overfitting (2026-09-23, later)

Everything above was tuned against one 6-month window on two assets
(IBIT/ETHA), both trending upward. Per the earlier "no backtest, ever"
gap analysis, that's a thin sample to trust a tuned parameter against.
Broadened the test: **GBTC** (Grayscale Bitcoin Trust, trading since
2015) gives real data across multiple market regimes — 2018 bear, 2022
crash, several bull runs — and real **Solana ETFs** (VSOL/BSOL/GSOL, live
since Jan 2025) give a genuinely different underlying asset.

**Important caveat:** GBTC/Solana-ETF data here is **daily** bars, not
hourly like IBIT/ETHA and the live strategy. This tests the 10/30-period
crossover *concept* at a different timescale (10-day/30-day, not
10h/30h), not the literal live strategy. `cooldown_bars=12` is reused
as-is since it's proportionally identical either way (12/30 = 0.4x the
long window, same ratio in both units) - not an arbitrary constant carried
over unscaled.

### Result: the entry filter + cooldown does NOT generalize

| Period | Raw return | Filtered return | Delta | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2018 bear | -30.46% | -32.98% | -2.51% | filter **hurt** |
| 2019-20 recovery | +50.78% | +152.09% | +101.31% | filter helped |
| 2021 choppy-bull | +31.32% | +30.05% | -1.27% | filter **hurt** |
| 2022 crash | -32.12% | -36.64% | -4.52% | filter **hurt** |
| 2023-24 bull | +62.22% | +43.58% | -18.63% | filter **hurt** |
| 2025-26 recent | +9.34% | -15.44% | -24.78% | filter **hurt** |

**The filter+cooldown helped in 1 of 6 regimes and hurt in 5 of 6.** The
one win (2019-20) is a large outlier; every other period shows the tuned
version underperforming the raw, untuned strategy - the opposite of what
the original IBIT/ETHA sweep suggested. The Solana ETF results
(2025-2026, a declining period) were similarly mixed: filter+cooldown
helped VSOL and BSOL, hurt GSOL.

### What this means

**The 0.25% entry threshold and 12h cooldown, as tuned, are very likely
overfit to the specific 6-month trending window they were picked from.**
They are not a validated general improvement to the strategy - they're a
good fit to one sample.

A separate, more robust pattern *did* emerge from the regime breakdown,
independent of the specific tuned values: **this class of strategy
(SMA crossover, trend-following) trails buy-and-hold badly in strong
secular trends** (2023-24: +43-62% vs buy&hold's +802.68%; 2019-20: even
the filtered version's +152% trails buy&hold's +592.64%) **and only
clearly earns its keep in choppy or declining markets** (2021 choppy-bull:
strategy positive, buy&hold -2.37%; 2018/2022 bear years: strategy loses
far less than buy&hold's near-total drawdowns). That tradeoff - giving up
upside in trends for downside protection in chop/bear markets - is
inherent to this strategy family, not a tuning artifact, and is worth
being explicit about when deciding whether to keep using it.

### Recommendation

Don't treat the current 0.25%/12h values as validated - they're a
reasonable starting point, not a proven edge. Options, in order of
rigor: widen the tuning sample (sweep against GBTC's multi-regime history
directly, not just IBIT/ETHA) before trusting a specific threshold;
simplify back toward the raw, untuned signal since the tuning didn't
clearly earn its added complexity; or accept the trend-following
tradeoff explicitly and stop expecting this strategy to beat buy-and-hold
in strong bull runs - only to protect capital better when trends don't
hold.

## Final re-tuning against the broader sample (2026-09-23, same day)

Swept `min_strength_pct` x `cooldown_bars` across all 11 series (IBIT,
ETHA, GBTC's 6 regime sub-periods, VSOL, BSOL, GSOL) jointly, scoring
each combination by how many series it beat its own raw (untuned)
baseline in, plus mean and worst-case delta vs raw across all 11 -
optimizing for robustness across regimes, not best-case fit to one.

| Setting | Regimes helped | Mean delta | Worst-case delta |
|---|---|---|---|
| Previous (0.25% / 12h) | 5/11 | +8.63% | -24.78% |
| **New (0% / 4h)** | **6/11** | **+13.23%** | **-10.30%** |

**0% strength (persistence-only) + 4h cooldown is the new default** -
`entry_filter.DEFAULT_MIN_STRENGTH_PCT=0`,
`position_state.DEFAULT_COOLDOWN_HOURS=4`. Better on every axis measured:
more regimes helped, higher average benefit, and a much safer worst case
(losing 10% of the raw strategy's return in the worst regime, vs losing
25% under the old tuning). A small non-zero strength value (0.1%) scored
almost identically and was not meaningfully better - simplicity favors 0%.

This doesn't mean the strategy is now "fixed" - it means the confirmation
+ cooldown mechanism generalizes reasonably well at these settings, while
the deeper finding from the broader-backtest section above still holds:
trend-following strategies give up upside in strong trends by design.
Nothing in this re-tuning changes that tradeoff, only how well the
whipsaw-filtering mechanism itself holds up across different markets.

## Concurrent-positions cap (2026-09-23, same day)

Every backtest above (`backtest.py`) simulates a single asset in
isolation - it has no notion of "how many assets are open at once," so
it can't evaluate `RISK_LIMITS["max_concurrent_positions"]`, a gate
flagged as an explicit out-of-scope gap when `volatility_sizing.py` was
built (that module sizes one position by its own volatility, not by
correlation with other simultaneously-held positions).

Built `portfolio_backtest.py`: a multi-asset simulator that runs the same
production entry/exit code (`entry_filter.confirmed_signal`,
`exit_criteria.check_exit`) across several price series at once, sharing
one cash pool, with each fresh entry sized at `max_position_pct` of
*total* portfolio value (mirroring `RiskManager.position_size`'s live
behavior) and an optional `max_concurrent_positions` cap that blocks a
new entry into a previously-flat asset once that many are already open -
existing positions still exit normally regardless of the cap. Requires
bar-aligned series (same calendar date/timestamp at each index across
all assets), since sizing and the cap only make sense when every asset's
price at index i is simultaneous.

### Test groups

Real crypto historicals don't exist (same limitation as every other
backtest in this file), and a meaningful correlation test additionally
needs several series that overlap in time, which most of the proxy data
gathered so far does not (the 6 GBTC regime slices, for instance, are
sequential, not simultaneous). Two groups in the existing cached data do
overlap:

- **Group A** - IBIT + ETHA, hourly, the full 732-bar overlapping window
  used throughout this file.
- **Group B** - GBTC (sliced to 2025-01-01–2026-09-22) + VSOL + BSOL +
  GSOL, daily, all four perfectly date-aligned (431 identical trading
  days) - a 4-way correlated group (all crypto-beta assets over the same
  recent, declining period).

Both groups ran with the already-tuned entry filter (0%), cooldown
(proportional to `long_window`), and exit levels (10%/15%/70%), varying
only `max_concurrent_positions` from 1 up to the group size (uncapped).

### Results

| Group A (IBIT/ETHA) | Return | Max drawdown | Trades | Peak concurrent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Uncapped (2) | +2.83% | 0.85% | 42 | 2 |
| Cap = 1 | +1.31% | 0.51% | 28 | 1 |
| Cap = 2 | +2.83% | 0.85% | 42 | 2 |

| Group B (GBTC/VSOL/BSOL/GSOL) | Return | Max drawdown | Trades | Peak concurrent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Uncapped (4) | -1.34% | 6.30% | 47 | 4 |
| Cap = 1 | +0.08% | 2.61% | 17 | 1 |
| Cap = 2 | -0.68% | 4.32% | 31 | 2 |
| Cap = 3 | -0.55% | 5.61% | 41 | 3 |
| Cap = 4 | -1.34% | 6.30% | 47 | 4 |

**Lower caps consistently reduced max drawdown in both groups**, and in
the more-correlated 4-asset group, capping to 1 also produced the best
*return* of any setting tested - the only case in this session where
adding a constraint improved both risk and return together, rather than
trading one for the other. The mechanism: correlated assets fire
near-duplicate signals close together in time, so holding several at
once mostly concentrates the same underlying bet (and its whipsaw
losses) rather than diversifying risk.

### Choosing a live default

Unlike the other tunings in this file, this sweep could only ever test 2
or 4 correlated series (no larger real, bar-aligned correlated group
exists in the gathered data), so it validates the *direction* (capping
helps) rather than a backtested-optimal number for the live 15-asset
watchlist, which has several distinct clusters (core BTC/ETH/SOL/DOGE,
the meme-coin group, PYTH/XLM) the sweep never had the data to represent
together. Extrapolating cap=1 (the sweep's best-performing setting) to
15 assets would effectively make the agent single-asset, which is a much
larger claim than this data supports.

Given that gap, the choice of live default was put to the account owner
rather than picked unilaterally (2026-09-23): **`RISK_LIMITS["max_concurrent_positions"] = 5`**
(about a third of the watchlist) - a moderate extension of the sweep's
direction that bounds the worst case (a systemic crypto-wide move
triggering many correlated signals at once, right before it reverses)
without assuming the effect keeps scaling all the way down to 1 across
clusters this sweep couldn't test. Enforced by
`RiskManager.can_open_new_position(open_position_count)`, wired into
`PLAYBOOK.md` alongside the whipsaw cooldown check (same scope: blocks
only fresh entries into previously-flat assets, never exits, never an
asset already held).

### Why RSI filtering doesn't fit this strategy

Both polarities fail for the same structural reason: this is a
trend-following strategy (SMA crossover), whose edge comes from catching
a move as momentum builds - which is precisely when RSI reads high.
Blocking "overbought" entries, or requiring a minimum RSI momentum
floor, both end up rejecting exactly the strong, early-trend crosses the
strategy exists to catch, especially costly in the recovery/crash
regimes (2019-20 recovery, 2022 crash) where the entries worth taking
are strong moves off an extreme.
